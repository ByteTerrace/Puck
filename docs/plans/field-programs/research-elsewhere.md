# How other SDF engines and tools compile, specialize, and cache their programs

This memo surveys how shipped signed-distance games, commercial sculpting tools, node-graph compilers, and academic implicit-surface systems evaluate, specialize, and cache geometric programs. It is the external survey for [the field programs design round](../field-programs.md), framed by Puck's settled rendering choices that [the frame does not interpret SDF programs](../../decisions/rendering.md#the-frame-does-not-interpret-sdf-programs) and that authored shading uses [interpret first, specialize behind](../../decisions/rendering.md#authored-shading).

The main finding is that **no shipped engine solves fast interactive editing, fast evaluation, and CPU–GPU parity with a single monolithic compiler**. Systems that stay interactive during sculpting either bake edits incrementally into local spatial bricks (*Dreams*, *Claybook*, *Substance 3D Modeler*), interpret a flat bytecode stream with aggressive per-region spatial pruning (*libfive*, *Fidget*, Keeter's massively parallel renderer, Zanni's synchronized tracing, and Barbier et al.'s *Lipschitz Pruning*), or generate specialized shaders behind a caching boundary (*Material Maker*, *Curv*, *Dr.Jit*). Across the entire survey, **spatial program reduction**—shrinking an object's program to only the operations active inside a lattice cell, brick, or tile—routinely delivers larger speedups (one to two orders of magnitude) than replacing an interpreter loop with straight-line code alone. At the same time, **generating every evaluator from one operation specification** is the only reliable way to keep GPU sampling, CPU point queries, interval bounds, and derivative walkers from drifting apart.

## How this survey handles sources

External claims below are cited to the SIGGRAPH, Eurographics, and GDC papers, course notes, repository documentation, and technical articles retrieved during this research round. Several commercial sculpting tools—*Womp*, *Unbound*, *MagicaCSG*, and parts of *Substance 3D Modeler*—are closed source and publish user documentation rather than engine internals; where their inner compilation or caching pipeline is not publicly documented, this memo marks those internals as unverified rather than inferring them from general WebGL or OpenGL patterns. Where talks or papers were accessed through conference archive pages and technical summaries rather than full slide transcripts or PDFs, the gaps are noted plainly. Every statement about Puck refers to the source files and symbols read in this repository.

## Shipped games and creation engines

### Media Molecule's Dreams

Alex Evans's SIGGRAPH 2015 presentation, *Learning from Failure: A Survey of Promising, Unconventional and Mostly Abandoned Renderers for "Dreams PS4"* ([Advances in Real-Time Rendering 2015](http://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2015/index.html)), remains the most thoroughly documented shipped SDF game engine architecture.

- **Interpreted or compiled:** Sculptures are stored as compact, non-destructive **edit lists**—sequential logs of 1 to more than 100,000 primitive CSG additions, subtractions, and smooth blends—rather than baked meshes or monolithic shaders. The engine does not compile a custom shader per sculpt. Instead, a chain of over 40 PS4 compute shaders (including heavy SDF evaluation shaders Evans called the "CS of doom," exceeding 3,000 instructions) interprets the edit list into spatial volume blocks and ultimately into multi-resolution point clouds ("flecks" or splats).
- **Specialized per object or per scene:** Evaluation is localized **per sculpt and per spatial brick**. Early prototypes ("the brick engine") partitioned space hierarchically into 4×4×4 blocks (matching the PS4's 64-wide GCN wavefront) and 8×8×8 distance/color bricks, culling the sculpt's edit list so each brick evaluates only the edits whose bounding regions touch it. The shipping renderer retained the edit-list-to-brick evaluator as its baking front end, then extracted screen-space splats from the evaluated bricks and rendered a bounding volume hierarchy of point clouds.
- **How edits stay interactive:** Because frames render cached point clouds rather than walking the edit list per pixel, frame cost is decoupled from sculpt history length. When an author stamps or modifies an edit, only the bricks touched by that edit's bounds re-evaluate and regenerate splats. Loading a downloaded creation replays its compact edit list on the GPU in fractions of a second to milliseconds, trading load-time compute for tiny network payloads.
- **Op representations and parity:** Technical summaries of Media Molecule's talks report that *Dreams* avoided third-party triangle-mesh physics middleware (such as Havok, PhysX, or Bullet) and derived collision penetration and surface normals directly from its volumetric SDF representation rather than separate triangle colliders. However, primary talk transcripts verifying whether *Dreams* ran a separate CPU-side edit-list interpreter or read back GPU-evaluated SDF bricks for physics were not accessible in full during this round; exact CPU–GPU op parity mechanisms in *Dreams* should be treated as unverified.
- **Published costs:** Evans documented 1 to 100,000+ edits per sculpt, compute shaders exceeding 3,000 instructions during brick evaluation, and 4×4×4 / 8×8×8 brick hierarchies on base PS4 hardware. Isolated millisecond timings for edit re-evaluation versus splat rendering were not verified from raw slide decks in this round.

### Second Order's Claybook

Sebastian Aaltonen's GDC 2018 presentation, *GPU-Based Clay Simulation and Ray-Tracing Tech in "Claybook"* ([GDC Vault](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1025030/Advanced-Graphics-Techniques-Tutorial-GPU)), shows the opposite pole from analytical raymarching: baking all CSG operations into a live GPU 3D texture volume.

- **Interpreted or compiled:** *Claybook* does not compile per-scene SDF shaders and does not interpret deep analytical trees per ray step. Complex shapes are offline-baked into 3D volume texture "brushes" ($32^3$ to $128^3$ resolution, 32 KB to 2 MB each, packed into a single atlas). At runtime, compute shaders combine brushes using rigid transforms, uniform scale, and smooth boolean operations (exponential min/max) into a global world SDF volume texture.
- **Specialized per object or per scene:** Work is specialized **per spatial tile** in the world volume. A first compute pass dispatches over a 64×64×32 brush grid (4×4×4 thread groups) to cull brushes into per-cell lists, a second pass compacts active tiles via atomics, and subsequent passes sparsely populate 8×8×8 level-0 bricks and their mip chain.
- **How edits stay interactive:** Deforming clay or stamping a new brush updates only the overlapping 8×8×8 tiles of the world SDF texture. Rendering (cone-tracing prepass, primary ray-tracing, soft shadows, and ambient occlusion) reads hardware-filtered 3D textures rather than re-evaluating the brush history.
- **Op representations and parity:** *Claybook* avoids the CPU–GPU parity problem by running **both rendering and clay/fluid physics on the GPU** against the same 3D volume texture. Particles and rolling clay bodies query trilinearly filtered distances and gradients directly from the GPU world SDF. Because there is no deterministic CPU simulation reading an analytical op stream, there is only one SDF evaluator.
- **Published costs:** The world SDF is a 1024×1024×512 8-bit signed 3D texture with 5 mip levels totaling **586 MB**, encoding distances in $[-4, +4]$ voxels for $1/32$-voxel sub-voxel precision. The GPU position-based fluid simulation runs in **under 1 ms** on consoles, leaving the remainder of the 16.6 ms (60 FPS) budget for sparse SDF updates and ray-tracing passes. The trade-off is clear: 586 MB covers a bounded tabletop playfield at uniform voxel resolution, which does not scale to open MMO landscapes or arbitrary analytical sharpness without sparse paging or a hybrid representation.

### Adobe Substance 3D Modeler

Adobe *Substance 3D Modeler* descends from Oculus Medium (later Adobe Medium), detailed in Adobe's product documentation ([Adobe Substance 3D Modeler](https://helpx.adobe.com/substance-3d-modeler.html)), coverage of its OpenVDB support ([80 Level](https://80.lv/)), and Oculus engineer David Farrell's VRDC 2019 talk *Sculpting and Simulations with 6DoF Controllers* ([VRDC 2019 summary](https://nosferalatu.com/) and [Meta engineering documentation](https://developers.meta.com/)).

- **Interpreted or compiled:** *Modeler* splits authoring into two representations that coexist in the same SDF workspace: non-destructive **parametric primitives** (analytical SDF equations with bevel, hollow, and combine parameters) and freeform **Clay** stored in sparse 3D SDF voxel grids (interoperable with OpenVDB `.vdb` volumes). Moving from parametric shapes to freeform sculpting uses an explicit one-way **Convert to Clay** bake that discretizes the analytical primitives into the local voxel grid.
- **Specialized per object or per scene:** Voxel resolution is managed **per layer and per sparse block**, allocating memory only in a narrow band around surfaces.
- **How edits stay interactive:** Freeform strokes modify only the touched sparse blocks (including elastic deformations via Kelvinlets in Oculus Medium). Rather than re-meshing an entire sculpture or compiling shaders on every brush stroke, Medium used adaptive local isosurface extraction (**Transvoxel**) to update preview geometry patches incrementally and hold 90+ FPS in VR, while also supporting direct volumetric workflows and mesh export.
- **Op representations and parity:** Proprietary internals are not published, so whether parametric primitives share a single code-generated definition between CPU meshing/export and GPU viewport evaluation is **unverified**.
- **Published costs:** Public talks emphasize sustaining VR frame budgets (90+ FPS) through localized sparse-grid edits and Transvoxel LOD transitions; no per-op evaluation timings or memory budgets for *Substance 3D Modeler* were published in retrieved sources.

### Womp, Unbound, and MagicaCSG

Three widely used commercial SDF tools illustrate how interactive editors choose different compromises around compilation, meshing, and physics, though all three are closed source and publish limited internal architecture details.

- **Womp** ([womp.com](https://womp.com/), founder profile at [f.inc](https://f.inc/)): A browser-native "Liquid 3D" modeling tool built on SDFs and GPU raymarching (WebGL transitioning toward WebGPU in browser graphics). Users manipulate non-destructive primitive lists with smooth unions, carves, and material blends interactively in the viewport and export meshes for external pipelines. **What could not be verified:** Womp has not published engineering documentation confirming whether its viewport runs a bytecode/buffer interpreter, compiles a specialized GLSL/WGSL shader per scene edit (using async shader compilation and uniform updates for drags), or caches subtrees into 3D bricks, nor how its mesh exporter shares op math with its GPU raymarcher.
- **Unbound** ([unbound.io](https://www.unbound.io/)): An SDF modeling tool and interactive game engine with an ordered, non-destructive **Edit** stack (primitives, carves, blends, mirrors, painters), embedded **Lua 5.3** scripting, and **Jolt Physics** integration. Notably for Puck's contact question, Unbound does not rely on a deterministic CPU SDF interpreter for gameplay physics: it feeds auto-generated or configured collision shapes derived from the SDF models into Jolt Physics, decoupling simulation collision from the live SDF viewport. **What could not be verified:** Unbound has not published technical talks detailing whether its GPU renderer interprets the edit stack, caches sparse bricks, or compiles specialized shaders, nor its frame or bake timings.
- **MagicaCSG** ([ephtracy.github.io](https://ephtracy.github.io/index.html?page=magicacsg), mesh workflow overview at [GameFromScratch](https://gamefromscratch.com/)): Built by the author of MagicaVoxel, MagicaCSG requires Windows 64-bit, OpenGL 4.6, and at least 2 GB of VRAM, and combines a non-destructive CSG/SDF hierarchy (primitives, sweeps, splines, mirrors, smooth blends, chamfers, grooves, instancing) with an interactive GPU path tracer, mesh export (`.ply`), and experimental Mesh SDF support. Crucially, MagicaCSG exposes **independent volume resolution per object** (`⊕` / `⊖` controls), which strongly indicates localized per-object spatial discretization or grid acceleration, and exported meshes show dense isosurface extraction that users commonly decimate in Blender. **What could not be verified:** ephtracy has not open-sourced MagicaCSG or published its internal GLSL architecture, so whether individual objects bake into sparse voxel bricks, evaluate a pruned CSG buffer per cell, or compile specialized shaders remains unverified.

## Shader-generating tools and SDF compilers

### Material Maker and graph-to-shader compilers

*Material Maker* ([RodZill4/material-maker](https://github.com/RodZill4/material-maker)), built on Godot, is an open-source reference for compiling user-authored visual graphs—including 2D and 3D signed distance fields (`sdf3d`)—directly into GLSL shaders.

- **Interpreted or compiled:** Fully **compiled to GLSL**. Each node emits GLSL code snippets (`generate_code` / `mm_gen_shader`), and the graph compiler walks upstream from outputs to stitch a unified shader. Unlike raster texture filters that pass pixel values between nodes, `sdf3d` nodes pass **distance functions** as inlined GLSL code expressions that feed into a compiled raymarching or map-baking shader.
- **Specialized per object or per scene:** Specialized **per graph segment**. Because inlining an entire complex procedural graph into one monolithic shader quickly causes exponential expression growth, register exhaustion, and multi-second driver compilation stalls or GPU timeouts, Material Maker introduces explicit **Buffer nodes** as compilation boundaries. A Buffer node renders its upstream sub-graph into a cached texture (in chunks if needed), and downstream nodes sample that texture instead of re-inlining the upstream math.
- **How edits stay interactive:** Changing a uniform parameter updates the running shader without recompiling; changing graph topology recompiles only the affected shader segment between Buffer boundaries.
- **Op representations and parity:** GPU-only (GLSL); there is no CPU evaluator that must match the shader.
- **Design lesson for Puck:** Material Maker confirms the exact failure mode Puck's [authored shading decision](../../decisions/rendering.md#authored-shading) already encountered: compiling arbitrary user graphs straight into one shader hits driver compile latency and register pressure unless compilation is either bounded/segmented or hidden behind an immediate interpreter.

### Curv and Bauble

Two open-source programmatic SDF environments show what happens when a language compiles directly to GPU shaders while also supporting CPU meshing.

- **Curv** ([curv3d/curv](https://github.com/curv3d/curv), by Doug Moen) compiles pure functional F-Rep / SDF programs into **GLSL fragment shaders** for interactive raymarching in its viewport, and exports watertight meshes (STL, OBJ, X3D) on the CPU. For fast CPU mesh export (`-Ojit`), Curv compiles the SDF expression to **C++ using GLM** and invokes the system C++ compiler (GCC/Clang) to produce native code. This keeps one authored language, but requires maintaining parallel GLSL and C++ emitters plus an external compiler toolchain—and because float math and compiler optimizations differ between GLSL drivers and host C++, it does not give cross-machine bit-identical determinism.
- **Bauble** ([ianthehenry/bauble](https://github.com/ianthehenry/bauble), by Ian Henry) lowers a Janet Lisp dialect of SDF combinators straight into **GLSL** for WebGL/WebAssembly live coding. Like Material Maker, its viewport compiles a specialized shader per structural edit and relies on GPU driver compilation speed rather than an interpreter.

### Inigo Quilez on SDF evaluation, exactness, and bounds

Inigo Quilez's reference articles ([Distance Functions](https://iquilezles.org/articles/distfunctions/), [Smooth Minimum](https://iquilezles.org/articles/smin/), [Interior Distance](https://iquilezles.org/articles/interiordistance/), [Domain Repetition](https://iquilezles.org/articles/sdfrepetition/), [SDF Bounding Volumes](https://iquilezles.org/articles/sdfbounding/), [Normals for an SDF](https://iquilezles.org/articles/normalsSDF/), and [3D Distance and Gradient Functions](https://iquilezles.org/articles/distgradfunctions3d/)) codify the mathematical constraints that dominate both GPU baking and CPU contact evaluation:

- **Exact distances versus lower bounds:** Primitives like spheres and boxes satisfy the Eikonal equation ($|\nabla f(p)| = 1$) everywhere, whereas non-uniform scales, space warps, polynomial/exponential smooth minimums (`smin`), and overlapping mirror folds violate $|\nabla f| = 1$ and yield only a **distance bound** once divided by their Lipschitz constant $L$. In a sphere tracer—or a bake whose global `stepScale` divides every sample—a single high-$L$ warp penalizes every query unless its factor is scoped locally.
- **Broken interior distance:** Even a hard union `min(a, b)` of two exact primitives breaks the interior distance field: inside the overlap, `min(a, b)` measures to the internal shared seam rather than the true outer surface. Quilez's *Interior Distance* note explains the exact artifact Puck's `SdfSampledLattice` encounters at touching boxes and thin sub-cell layers, where interior gradients point toward internal faces rather than the exterior contour.
- **Bounding volumes before heavy shapes:** Quilez demonstrates testing a cheap outer bounding primitive (sphere or box) and skipping an expensive child SDF whenever `d_bound > d_current`. In a compiled or interpreted scene, per-subtree bounding tests turn $O(N)$ per-sample evaluation into $O(\text{local overlap})$ without changing the surface.
- **Analytical gradients versus tetrahedral finite differences:** Computing normals via 4-tap tetrahedral finite differences multiplies field evaluations by 4 (or 5 when paired with the center sample, as in Puck's `sdf-mesh-bake.comp.hlsl`). Quilez's *3D Distance and Gradient Functions* show that propagating analytical $(d, \nabla d)$ tuples (forward-mode automatic differentiation) through primitives and smooth blends computes both value and exact gradient in a single pass with zero finite-difference epsilon tuning and no crease-straddling tap artifacts—a direct counterpart to Keeter's `Grad` evaluator below.

## Academic work on compiling and pruning implicit surfaces

### libfive, Keeter (SIGGRAPH 2020), and Fidget

Matt Keeter's three-generation body of work—**libfive** ([libfive.com](https://libfive.com/), [github.com/libfive/libfive](https://github.com/libfive/libfive)), his SIGGRAPH 2020 paper *Massively Parallel Rendering of Complex Closed-Form Implicit Surfaces* ([mattkeeter.com/research/mpr](https://www.mattkeeter.com/research/mpr/)), and his Rust library **Fidget** ([github.com/mkeeter/fidget](https://github.com/mkeeter/fidget))—is the single closest academic match to Puck's field-program problem.

- **Interpreted, JIT-compiled, or shader-compiled:** Keeter explicitly rejects compiling a new GPU shader per shape because shader compilation stalls interactive editing. Instead, an expression DAG is deduplicated, constant-folded, and lowered to a linear SSA **instruction tape** (a `Deck` in *libfive*, a register-allocated bytecode tape in MPR and *Fidget*). On the GPU (MPR), a fixed CUDA kernel interprets that tape. On the CPU (*Fidget*), the same SSA tape targets either a portable bytecode VM (`VmFunction`) using 256 virtual registers (with `LOAD`/`STORE` spills from a deterministic single-pass allocator) or a hand-written **native JIT compiler** (`JitFunction`) emitting straight-line `x86_64` (AVX2) or `AArch64` (NEON) machine code directly into executable memory in microseconds, with no LLVM dependency.
- **Specialized per spatial cell (tape pruning / shortening):** Keeter's breakthrough is **spatial specialization of the tape itself**. Evaluating the tape over a coarse spatial tile or octree cell using **interval arithmetic** yields `[min, max]` bounds for every node. Whenever a `min(a, b)` or `max(a, b)` node has non-overlapping child intervals (for example `a.max < b.min`), the losing branch is provably dead everywhere in that cell. A reverse pass over the tape strips dead clauses and writes a **shortened tape** for that cell's children. By the time evaluation reaches leaf pixels or voxels, tapes of thousands of operations shrink by orders of magnitude—often to a handful of ops—so every sample in a region pays only for the geometry actually near it.
- **How edits stay interactive:** Editing a shape rebuilds the compact SSA tape in microseconds and uploads it as data; there is no driver shader compilation. In *Fidget*, custom native JIT emission avoids both shader-compiler and LLVM startup latency.
- **Op representations and parity:**
  - *libfive* defines all scalar/CSG operations in one central `Opcode` table (X-macro style) consumed by `IntervalEvaluator`, `ArrayEvaluator`, and derivative/feature evaluators over the same `Deck`. Adding an op still requires implementing its math rule in each evaluator class, but the opcode list and tape layout have one source of truth.
  - *Fidget* goes further by structuring evaluation around unified Rust traits (`Function`, `TracingEvaluator`) across four modes over one opcode set: scalar/interval evaluation (`Interval`), forward-mode automatic differentiation (`Grad`), and bulk SIMD slices (`FloatSliceEval`, `GradSliceEval`).
- **Published costs:** In Keeter's SIGGRAPH 2020 paper (CUDA on an NVIDIA GTX 1080 Ti), brute-force GPU tape interpretation took roughly **58 ms per megapixel**, whereas hierarchical interval evaluation with on-the-fly GPU tape shortening dropped complex scenes **below 8 ms** (an order-of-magnitude reduction from pruning alone, while still running an interpreter). In *Fidget*'s benchmarks on the 7,866-operation *Prospero* benchmark (1024×1024 grid), combining tape shortening with custom native SIMD JIT cuts brute-force multi-second evaluation down to single-digit milliseconds—bringing an Apple M1 Max CPU JIT within reach of a GTX 1080 Ti running MPR.

### Affine arithmetic and range analysis (Sharp & Jacobson, SIGGRAPH 2022)

Nicholas Sharp and Alec Jacobson's SIGGRAPH 2022 Best Paper, *Spelunking the Deep: Guaranteed Queries on General Neural Implicit Surfaces via Range Analysis* ([arXiv:2202.02464](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.02464)), highlights a subtle limitation of standard interval arithmetic that Puck already feels in `SdfFieldEvaluator.Bounds.cs`.

- **The dependency problem:** Standard interval arithmetic treats every occurrence of $x$, $y$, or $z$ as independent. Whenever a coordinate is rotated, folded, or reused in a formula like $x^2 - x$, interval bounds artificially bloat ("wrapping effect"). Puck's own `SdfFieldEvaluator.Bounds.cs` remarks note exactly this: rotations and folds widen interval hulls significantly.
- **Affine arithmetic on GPU:** Sharp and Jacobson propagate **affine forms** ($a_0 + \sum a_i \epsilon_i + \text{err}$) through implicit evaluation graphs on the GPU, preserving first-order linear correlations across coordinate transforms and linear combinations. Because affine bounds stay much tighter than raw intervals over rotated boxes and deep expressions, spatial subdivision and marching-cubes extraction prune dramatically more empty cells—even for general implicit fields that are not 1-Lipschitz SDFs.
- **Relevance to Puck:** Affine arithmetic is more expensive per op than plain intervals (carrying $x, y, z$ linear coefficients plus an error term), so it pays off specifically at coarse subdivision levels (such as lattice slab culling or certified sweep intervals across rotated shapes), not at every fine leaf sample.

### Synchronized tracing and Lipschitz Pruning (Zanni 2023; Barbier et al., Eurographics 2025)

Two recent GPU papers target analytical CSG/BlobTree scenes with thousands of primitives where neither offline voxel baking nor per-scene shader compilation is viable:

- **Synchronized-tracing of Implicit Surfaces** (Cédric Zanni, *ACM TOG* / [arXiv:2304.09673](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09673)): Eliminates offline BVH rebuilds so artists can drag primitives interactively. A low-resolution screen-tile A-buffer tracks primitives intersecting each tile, threads in a workgroup synchronize through shared memory for coherent evaluation, and a **sparse bottom-up tree traversal prunes the BlobTree on the fly** so evaluation cost decouples from total tree size.
- **Lipschitz Pruning: Hierarchical Simplification of Primitive-Based SDFs** (Wilhem Barbier, Mathieu Sanchez, Axel Paris, Élie Michel, Thibaud Lambert, Tamy Boubekeur, Mathias Paulin, and Théo Thonat, *Computer Graphics Forum* / Eurographics 2025 Best Paper Honorable Mention, [wbrbr.org](https://wbrbr.org/)): Instead of compiling a CSG tree to a shader (which can stall for tens of seconds on thousands of primitives) or evaluating the full tree per step, compute shaders run a coarse-to-fine spatial hierarchy each frame using the **Lipschitz property** of SDFs. Inside any bounding ball or cell of radius $r$ around center $c$, a subtree with distance $f(c)$ and Lipschitz constant $L$ is bounded by $[f(c) - L r,\, f(c) + L r]$. Using those bounds, binary CSG nodes collapse to a single active child or to a constant lower-bound expression for that cell.
- **Published costs:** Barbier et al. report up to a **629× speedup** over unoptimized sphere tracing on dense analytical scenes with over 6,000 nodes. On a laptop RTX 4060, full dynamic pruning and tracing run interactively in the millisecond range (roughly **14.3 ms** for pruning and **15.2 ms** for sphere tracing in their complex benchmark), rebuilt from scratch every frame with no shader compilation and no static BVH bake.
- **Key insight for Puck's bake:** Unlike interval arithmetic—which requires a separate interval implementation for every primitive and warp and suffers from rotation wrapping—**Lipschitz pruning needs only a center evaluation plus each node's Lipschitz bound** (which `SdfProgram.Lipschitz.cs` and `SdfFieldEvaluator.Certificates.cs` already compute). For a 3D bake lattice, pruning an instance's instruction stream per 8×8×8 coarse brick using center-plus-Lipschitz bounds works cleanly with arbitrary smooth blends, warps, and reliefs.

### Multi-target JIT and shader specialization (Dr.Jit, Selos, LuisaRender)

General graphics compilation frameworks show the ceiling—and the cost—of true compilation:

- **Dr.Jit** (Wenzel Jakob et al., SIGGRAPH 2022, [rgl.epfl.ch](https://rgl.epfl.ch/publications/Jakob2022DrJit)): Records a dynamic computation graph from high-level code and JIT-compiles specialized data-parallel megakernels for both **CPU (LLVM)** and **GPU (PTX/OptiX)**, including forward- and reverse-mode automatic differentiation. It proves that one graph IR can drive both CPU and GPU fast paths with zero interpreter dispatch, at the cost of heavy compiler infrastructure and compile latency that must be amortized across many evaluations.
- **Shader metaprogramming (*Selos* and *LuisaRender*):** Multi-stage specialization systems such as *Selos* ([SIGGRAPH shader specialization](https://www.siggraph.org/)) and *LuisaRender* ([Tsinghua graphics publications](https://www.tsinghua.edu.cn/)) remove dynamic branching and register bloat from interpreters through compile-time or link-time specialization. That is the same pattern Puck already adopted for authored shading (`SdfShadeOps.Table` and `SdfShadeHlsl` generating both an immediate interpreter and a background-compiled specialized HLSL kernel). Across Unreal ([the author-shaders engine survey](../author-shaders/engines.md)), *Material Maker*, *Curv*, and *Lipschitz Pruning*, the compile-time trade-off is consistent: GPU shader compilation takes hundreds of milliseconds to seconds, so **specialized shaders can accelerate steady-state or repeated heavy work (like cold load-time bakes of complex worlds or pre-warmed player builds), never the first-frame response to a sculptor's edit**.

## Where sources disagree

Three recurring engineering disagreements appear across the surveyed systems, and each one maps directly to a design choice in Puck:

- **Compiling specialized GPU shaders versus pruning interpreted programs.** *Material Maker*, *Curv*, and *Bauble* compile user SDF graphs directly into GLSL shaders, accepting driver compilation latency on structural edits (and relying on explicit `Buffer` cache nodes in *Material Maker* when shaders grow too large). Keeter (SIGGRAPH 2020), Zanni (2023), and Barbier et al. (Eurographics 2025) argue that per-model GPU shader compilation fails for complex interactive scenes—where driver compilation stalls for seconds to tens of seconds—and demonstrate one to two orders of magnitude speedups by pruning interpreted bytecode or trees per spatial tile instead.
- **Interval arithmetic versus affine arithmetic versus Lipschitz bounds for spatial pruning.** Keeter (*libfive*, MPR, *Fidget*) uses plain interval arithmetic to prune arbitrary closed-form expressions without needing Lipschitz constants. Sharp and Jacobson (SIGGRAPH 2022) argue that plain intervals suffer severe wrapping bloat under coordinate reuse and rotations, favoring affine arithmetic for tighter pruning. Barbier et al. (Eurographics 2025) take a third route for SDF primitives: when each node already carries a Lipschitz constant, center evaluation plus a Lipschitz radius avoids both interval wrapping and affine-form storage overhead on the GPU.
- **Physics collision over SDFs.** *Claybook* runs rendering and clay/fluid physics against the same GPU 3D texture, *Unbound* exports derived shapes to Jolt Physics, and secondary accounts of *Dreams* describe custom volumetric SDF collision without third-party polygon middleware (though primary transcripts verifying whether *Dreams* ran a CPU edit-list twin or GPU readback were not accessible in full). None of the surveyed game engines combine GPU mesh baking with bit-identical fixed-point CPU contact over the same analytical program, which is why Puck's single-source op discipline matters more here than in any single-target engine.

## What fits Puck's architecture

Puck occupies a distinct point in the design space that none of the surveyed systems share completely:

1. **Frames never interpret SDFs.** Rendering rasterizes dual-contoured meshes cached in `SdfMeshStore`, baked at load and edit time (`SdfMeshPathBake`, `SdfSampledLattice`, `SdfDualContouring`). That immediately rules out *Claybook*-style 586 MB global 3D textures and *Dreams*-style splat renderers for the frame—Puck's mesh pipeline already beats them on memory scaling and crisp material/shadow composition.
2. **Cold bake time and high-Lipschitz creations are the GPU bottleneck.** Today `sdf-mesh-bake.comp.hlsl` calls the monolithic `mapCore` interpreter per lattice sample—and five times per gradient point (center plus four tetrahedral taps)—with only coarse per-lattice instance masks (`sdf-mesh-bake-mask.comp.hlsl`). A complex creation with reliefs, masks, or dozens of shapes walks every instruction of its active instances at every sample, even when only one or two shapes can affect that corner of the lattice.
3. **CPU contact queries must be bit-identical and certified.** Unlike *Claybook* (GPU-only physics) or *Unbound* (Jolt hulls), Puck's `SdfFieldEvaluator` must answer exact deterministic fixed-point (`FixedQ4816`) point distances and outward-rounded interval bounds (`SdfFieldEvaluator.Bounds.cs`) on the CPU for certified sweeps (`FixedFieldContactSolver.ResolveSweep`), across machines and backends.
4. **Two hand-written interpreters plus roughly ten CPU walkers are a maintenance and bug factory.** Every op currently exists as hand-synced HLSL in `mapCore`, hand-synced fixed-point C# in `SdfFieldEvaluator`, hand-synced interval C# in `SdfFieldEvaluator.Bounds.cs`, and switch arms across bounds, Lipschitz, certificates, lane contracts, and rigid plans.

### Weighing Puck-native combinations beside borrowed techniques

Comparing the external techniques against what Puck already built reveals four high-leverage combinations:

#### 1. One op table generating float HLSL, fixed-point C#, interval C#, and walker metadata (borrowed from *libfive*/*Fidget* + Puck's `SdfShadeOps.Table`)

*Fidget* and *libfive* prove that keeping point, interval, derivative, and slice evaluators aligned requires **one declarative op catalog**. Puck already proved this exact pattern for shading in `SdfShadeOps.Table` and `SdfShadeHlsl` (where one op table generates both the interpreter and specialized HLSL, guarded by `puck shaders generate --check` and a semantics fingerprint).

The Puck-native twist is that a field op has **four** consumers, not two: GPU `float` HLSL (`mapCore` and any specialized bake kernel), CPU `FixedQ4816` point evaluation, CPU `FixedInterval` bounds evaluation, and static analyses (Lipschitz factor, reach certificate, bound algebra, lane contract). Extending a single op specification—whether via a C# source generator over a typed arithmetic IR or an algebraic op table with explicit carrier hooks where fixed-point rounding differs—eliminates the silent CPU/GPU drift hazard (superellipsoid, capsule, relief-mask arms) at compile time. Where an op's fixed-point rounding or carrier guard cannot be synthesized trivially from float expressions (such as quantized quaternion rotation slack in `SdfFieldEvaluator.Certificates.cs` or `SdfWallpaperFold`), the generator should require an explicit fixed-point/interval arm on that single op record so no op can ever be added to one reader without the compiler demanding every other reader's arm.

#### 2. Per-brick / per-slab program pruning before baking (borrowed from Keeter 2020 and Barbier et al. 2025 *Lipschitz Pruning*, paired with Puck's `SdfMeshPathBake` slabs and `SdfFieldEvaluator.Certificates`)

This is likely the highest-dividend technique in the entire survey for Puck's bake—and it works **even before** compiling specialized bake shaders. Today `sdf-mesh-bake-mask.comp.hlsl` builds a bitmask of whole instances per lattice, not per brick or per shape inside an instance. Yet a large terrain sector or sculpted creation (like the north-west valley wall or a detailed character) has tens or hundreds of shapes, relief masks, and blends, while any 8×8×8 sub-brick of its final lattice touches only a tiny subset of them.

A Puck-native combination pairs **Barbier et al.'s Lipschitz pruning** with **Keeter's shortened tapes** over Puck's existing bake slabs:
- Evaluate each coarse cell (or 8×8×8 brick) once at its center with each shape/scope's known Lipschitz bound (which `SdfProgram.Lipschitz.cs` already computes, without needing interval arithmetic on the GPU).
- Prune dead union/subtraction branches, dead relief masks (where mask weight is provably zero across the brick, skipping noise evaluation completely), and dead shapes whose lower bound exceeds the brick's upper bound on the winning distance.
- Emit a compact per-brick **shortened instruction list** (or active-instruction bitset) that the fine lattice samples, crossing root brackets (up to eleven rounds), and 5-tap gradient batches all reuse.

Notice how well this fits Puck's multi-round bake: crossing root refinement (`SdfSampledLattice.CrossingPoints`) and tetrahedral gradients re-evaluate the field many times in the exact same small cells. Pruning once per coarse brick amortizes across coarse refinement, crossing bisection, and gradient taps—and costs zero shader compilation time, keeping edits instant.

#### 3. Interpret first, specialize behind—only if `field-measure` shows interpreter dispatch dominates after pruning (borrowed from *Dr.Jit* / *Material Maker* + Puck's `SdfShadeProgram` tiering)

The survey gives a sharp warning about compiling specialized GPU shaders per SDF object: *Material Maker*, *Curv*, Unreal, and *Lipschitz Pruning* all report that GPU shader compilation takes hundreds of milliseconds to tens of seconds. For a world with hundreds of distinct creation groups (`SdfMeshPathBake`), compiling one HLSL bake kernel per creation during a cold bake would easily **increase** total wall-clock bake time instead of reducing it, because DXC and driver compilation time would swamp a few milliseconds of GPU dispatch savings.

Specializing a GPU bake kernel only pays off in two narrow cases:
- **Heavy, sample-rich creations** (such as multi-million-sample terrain sectors or heavily relieved shapes where one group spends hundreds of milliseconds in `sdf-mesh-bake.comp.hlsl`), compiled asynchronously or cached by canonical field structure (just like `SdfShadeProgram` separates structure from runtime parameters so two creations with different transforms/radii share one kernel).
- **Pre-warmed player builds (`world prepare`)**, where background compilation is paid once at ship time.

Crucially, the sibling lane `field-measure` is measuring right now how much of a cold debug-world bake is interpreter overhead versus sample count and hand-specialized speedup. Until those numbers are in hand, per-brick pruning (which shrinks both interpreted and compiled work with zero compile latency) is strictly safer than jumping straight to per-creation HLSL codegen.

#### 4. Analytical forward-mode gradients for baking and compiled/JITted CPU contact evaluators (borrowed from Quilez and *Fidget*)

Two secondary findings from the survey fit Puck's dual GPU-bake / CPU-contact workload directly:
- **Forward-mode automatic differentiation (`Grad`, as in *Fidget* and Quilez's `distgradfunctions3d`):** Today `sdf-mesh-bake.comp.hlsl` computes every gradient with **5 full `mapCore` walks** (1 center + 4 tetrahedral taps). Evaluating $(d, \nabla d)$ analytically in a single walk cuts gradient sampling work dramatically, eliminates finite-difference step-size artifacts near creases, and fits cleanly if generated from the same single-source op table. (Noise/relief ops can still fall back to finite differences or analytical noise derivatives locally without losing the win on primitives and blends.)
- **CPU fixed-point JIT or IL emission for hot contact fields (borrowed from *Fidget* on .NET):** Unlike GPU shaders, .NET can emit lightweight IL (`DynamicMethod`) or specialized delegate pipelines for a static world's contact field in milliseconds with zero external toolchain—and unlike float JITs, integer `FixedQ4816` operations are bit-for-bit identical between an interpreter and emitted IL. However, Puck's `SdfFieldEvaluator` already prunes instances and segment groups via certified reach bounds (`SdfFieldEvaluator.Certificates.cs`, `SdfFieldEvaluator.Cull.cs`). Before adding a CPU JIT, profiling should confirm whether CPU contact evaluation is actually dispatch-bound or whether tighter bounds (e.g., selective **affine arithmetic** from Sharp & Jacobson at rotated scopes to cut unproved sweeps and interval bloat) buy more.

## Invariants, limits, and open questions

If Puck adopts single-source op generation and spatial brick pruning for field programs, three invariants govern correctness:

1. **Single-source op parity:** Every `SdfOp`, `SdfShapeType`, and `SdfBlendOp` is defined in one authoritative table or IR, and the build refuses any op missing an arm in GPU float evaluation, CPU fixed-point evaluation, CPU interval evaluation, or static bounds/Lipschitz analysis. Where fixed-point rounding differs from float, the discrete topology (shape/material winner, admission, skip decisions) matches and float distances agree within the documented scale tolerance (`SdfFieldDeviceLawTests`). *Enforced by:* build-time code generation plus `puck shaders generate --check`, owned by the `field-ops` lane. *Where the guarantee stops:* GPU drivers may still reassociate non-`precise` float arithmetic; GPU baking remains presentation-only and never feeds simulation state.
2. **Conservative spatial pruning:** A pruned brick program may omit an instruction, relief mask, or subtree only when a sound bound (Lipschitz center-radius bound or outward-rounded interval) proves it cannot change the winning distance or material at any point in the brick (including the tetrahedral tap halo if gradients share the brick). *Enforced by:* a shared conservative pruning pass tested by comparing pruned vs unpruned `mapCore` answers across random points in every brick. *Where the guarantee stops:* an under-estimated Lipschitz constant in an op definition breaks pruning soundness—making single-source Lipschitz definitions coupled directly to the op table essential.
3. **Deterministic CPU simulation:** Any CPU specialization (such as pruning static contact programs per spatial cell or emitting .NET IL for `SdfFieldEvaluator`) must produce bit-identical `FixedQ4816` results to the reference interpreter on every machine. *Enforced by:* fixed-point integer arithmetic laws and `puck test --reproduce`.

### Open questions owned by other lanes or follow-ups

- **Interpreter overhead vs sample count on Puck's cold bake:** Owned by sibling lane `field-measure`. Its measurements will decide whether specialized GPU bake shaders pay for their DXC/driver compile cost after spatial pruning, or whether the GPU interpreter over pruned per-brick tapes is already fast enough.
- **Single-source op architecture across C# fixed-point, C# intervals, and HLSL:** Owned by sibling lane `field-ops`.
- **Incremental dirty-region re-baking during live sculpting edits:** Not yet owned by a lane in this round. When the editor plan (`docs/plans/editor.md`) adds live sculpting, adopting *Dreams*/*Claybook*'s dirty-brick invalidation (re-sampling and re-contouring only the `SdfMeshChunks` / lattice cells overlapping the edited shape's old and new bounds, rather than re-baking the whole creation group) will be needed to keep edits interactive.
- **Affine arithmetic for rotated fixed-point contact boxes:** Follow-up for certified field contact (`docs/plans/open-items.md#certified-field-contact`) to measure whether affine enclosures reduce `physics.contact.field-unproved` and box-walk instruction counts on rotated creations enough to justify their per-op cost.

## Three recommendations for the synthesis

1. **Generate every field reader from one op specification first (`field-ops`), mirroring `SdfShadeOps.Table` and *Fidget*.** Before optimizing either interpreter, unify GPU `mapCore`, CPU `SdfFieldEvaluator` (point and interval), Lipschitz factors, and reach certificates under one op table and generator so no op can exist in one reader without the others. This eliminates the recurring CPU–GPU divergence hazard and creates the single point of leverage needed for every later optimization (pruning, analytical gradients, or specialization).
2. **Add per-brick Lipschitz/interval program pruning to the bake pipeline before building a per-creation GPU shader compiler.** Borrowing from Keeter (SIGGRAPH 2020) and Barbier et al. (*Lipschitz Pruning*, Eurographics 2025), prune each creation's instruction stream and relief masks per coarse 8×8×8 brick using center evaluation plus Lipschitz bounds, and reuse the shortened per-brick tape across fine refinement, crossing-root bisection, and gradient taps. This attacks sample evaluation cost by an order of magnitude with zero shader-compilation stall and keeps live editing interactive.
3. **Gate specialized GPU bake shaders strictly on `field-measure`'s numbers, and evaluate analytical gradients alongside them.** Only build background-compiled specialized bake kernels (using `SdfShadeProgram`'s "interpret first, specialize behind" and structure-only cache keys) if `field-measure` shows that interpreter dispatch remains a major share of cold bake time even after pruning—and restrict specialization to sample-heavy groups or `world prepare` caches so DXC/driver compile time never slows down the cold-bake iteration loop. In parallel, evaluate single-pass analytical gradients (`distgradfunctions3d` / *Fidget* `Grad`) for the bake to replace 5-tap tetrahedral walks where ops support exact derivatives.
